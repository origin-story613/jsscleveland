Judaism does not ask mourners to move on quickly. Instead, it offers a series of stages, each with its own customs, that let grief be expressed and then slowly ease back into daily life. Your rabbi can guide you on the details for your family.

## Before the funeral (aninut)

From the time of death until the burial, the close relatives are called *onenim*. Their only obligation is to attend to the funeral and burial, and they are exempt from many other religious obligations during this time.

## Shiva: the first seven days

After the burial, the mourners return home to sit shiva for seven days. Friends and community visit to comfort them, prayer services are often held in the home, and mourners refrain from work and ordinary activities. [Shiva guide and downloads]({{ '/resources/shiva-downloads/' | relative_url }})

## Shloshim: the first thirty days

After shiva, mourners gradually return to daily life, but some restrictions continue until the thirtieth day after the burial.

## The first year

For a parent, mourning continues for twelve months, and Kaddish is recited for eleven months. The Chevra Kadisha can [arrange for Kaddish]({{ '/resources/yahrtzeit/' | relative_url }}#kaddish--remembrance-services) to be recited on behalf of your loved one.

## Matzeiva (the headstone)

A headstone is placed on the grave, often with a short unveiling ceremony, within the first year. Customs on timing vary; your rabbi can advise you.

## Yahrtzeit

Each year, on the Hebrew date of the passing, the family lights a memorial candle and recites Kaddish. [Calculate a yahrtzeit date]({{ '/resources/yahrtzeit/' | relative_url }})
